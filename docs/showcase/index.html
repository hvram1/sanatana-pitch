The handful

Every living tradition is carried by a few people at a time.

A vidvān who knows the text by heart. A few students. A reciter whose voice the others learned from. Someone who keeps the files. For years, what the digital age gave them was this: the book, scanned.

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 1.1.1, इषे त्वोर्जे त्वा. Drag across.

A scan holds the words. It cannot hold the chant, the commentary beside the verse, or the teacher's voice. To hold those, a handful of people would need a team: an engineer, an editor, a designer, an analyst. A small tradition has never had one.

This year, one joined us. Claude Code.

The engineerwho finds what no one reported
yajurveda · 17 september
Trustee

how did u figure out the audio was corrupted and how were you able to recut . that seems a mystery to me

Claude Code

Chanting speed is steady, so text length predicts audio length. I counted, for all 648 anuvākas, the text characters per second of audio. The typical value is 7.4.

anuvākaaudiochars / s
A06232 s8.5
A077 s85.6
A0881 s13.7
A09161 s3.4
A07–A13 together798 s6.9

Each file on its own was wrong, but all seven together had the right amount of audio. So nothing was missing; the cuts between them were misplaced.

None of the new cuts has been heard. Listening to the start of 1.7.8 and 1.7.13 on the local page would confirm them.

Yes, that's what I'm suggesting, and it's built: mbh_src/qa/witness_check.py checks all 18 parvas in about 3 minutes and flagged 4,837 likely errors. It isn't a general spell checker. How good it is, measured against your editors' own fixes. … It flagged 42 of the 101 corrections made since. In 40 of those 42, the two editions' reading is exactly what the editor typed.

Of 40 random flags from today's XML, about 25 are real typos, for example

धुर्वेद → धनुर्वेद · मत्रयन्ति → मन्त्रयन्ति · वेदपरागः → वेदपारगः · महारजो → महाराजो

The analystwho shapes the work around the people doing it
reviewing the mahābhārata findings · 30 september
Trustee

we were looknig at the @qa/findings and had produced a html artefact. There are 2 ways to go about it. We could have somebody review the artefact by looking at the "corrected" / "not an erpr" . But that will require fixing a lot of javascript stuff. (e.g.) I keep clicknig on the Corrected button and the count just increases . The other option is to raise 5357 PR's against the mbh_src and somebody could apporve or reject . Does that make sense at all ?

Claude Code

It makes sense to ask, but I'd advise against 5,357 PRs. Fixing the review page is the smaller job.

  • Rejections get lost. Closing a PR as "not an error" leaves that decision in GitHub's history … so the witness pass doesn't raise the same finding again next run.
  • Many proposed fixes point the wrong way. … For example, the very first finding would "correct" कुलपतेर्द्वादशवार्षिके to कुलपतेर्दादशवार्षिक. A PR presents the reference reading as the fix, which pushes reviewers toward merging bad changes.
  • Reviewers see less. A PR diff shows one changed line. It doesn't show the witness IDs, the CE and Dutt readings side by side, or the tier, all of which the review page can show.

Sort by tier. Show both (CE and Dutt agree) first, since those are the likeliest real errors.

Trustee

i am saying people can do both . Some do online and some want to do on phone and email the stuff.

Claude Code

Design for touch. Big Corrected / Not an error buttons, the two readings stacked on top of each other, and nothing that needs a mouse. The online and offline versions will share one codebase, one decision format and one merge-and-apply script.

Which reviewer should I build and test with first: an online one, or someone on a phone using email?

The handful grows

The story we are proudest of.

The only edition of the Jaiminīya Sāmaveda was set in a proprietary font. On a computer, its pages were pictures of text: they could not be searched, copied or corrected.

We began there, with Claude Code: the font decoded character by character, and the text brought out in Devanāgarī, Grantha, Tamil and Malayalam. A vidvān and the Veda pāṭhaśālā in Palakkad proofread it svara by svara, filing their corrections as GitHub issues, 369 of them. We delivered an alpha version.

Then something better happened. Sri Sekhar Narayanaswamy, who lives there, saw what these tools made possible. He learned them, took the work over, and maintains it today.

That is the effect we hope for most.Not that a tool does the work, but that it makes the work easy enough to learn: someone new joins the handful, and the tradition continues.

There are thousands of handfuls like ours.

In maṭhas and pāṭhaśālās, in families of reciters, in every language of India. Each one is keeping a text, or a way of chanting it, alive.

Somewhere today a teacher is explaining a Purāṇa, and partway through will chant a verse. That verse deserves its address.
