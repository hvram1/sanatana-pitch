Sanatana Sampattiसनातनसम्पत्तिः

We put the foundational texts of Sanātana Dharma, their commentaries, and recordings of their recitation on the web, in a form anyone can read, cite, search and listen to.

Most "preserve our heritage" projects are PDF dumps with search. Ours are working sites, and every one below can be opened today. Six of the nine are public betas: they work, but their text, links and figures are still going through verification and cleanup before we call them published editions. Each one links to a page we chose to show the work at its best, so you can click through and judge for yourself.

Vedic Abhyāsa

Not yet public

A chanting practice tool. The student chants a line, and the app compares it with the ācārya's recording syllable by syllable. It shows which svaras went higher or lower than they should, and lets the student hear the difference.

  • Śrī Rudram and Chamakam in all four pāṭhas (saṃhitā, krama, jaṭā, ghana). That is 1,468 ghana lines for Rudram and 1,182 for Chamakam, with the ācārya's ghana class already labelled.

Section IV · Open problems, for collaboration

Four open problems we are working on, and would like to work on with others

These are research problems, not bugs. Each is specific to Indic texts and recordings, there is no commercial pull to solve it, and we have measured where today's methods stop. We are working on all four, and we would welcome collaborators: a university lab, a research group or individual researchers who want to work on them alongside us. The measurements are our own, taken in September and October 2026.

1

A "Shazam" for Vedic chant audio

Hold a phone up to anyone chanting the Veda and be told which mantra it is, in about 15 seconds, on the phone itself, with no cloud speech service. The chanter can be anyone, not only the people in our own recordings.

The text side is solved. Ten seconds of correctly heard chant names its place in the Taittirīya Saṃhitā or the Ṛgveda about 97% of the time. The difficulty is hearing the chant: turning a new chanter's voice into something that matches the text. We tried five approaches on the same clips:

Audio fingerprints (how Shazam works)140 / 140the same recording, even after a phone-call chain and added noise
0 / 104the same text, a different chanter. Fingerprints recognise recordings, not mantras.
Speech model hears letters, then text search36 / 39right answers on one clear voice
29 / 40clips of a group chanting the whole Rudram in which it heard almost nothing
Best music "cover-song" model (2025)21 / 104chance is about 7. A chant by another voice looks almost unrelated to it.

Two more approaches, which compare the sound shapes directly, sit between these. The four approaches other than fingerprints all work on the clear voice and all fail on the group recording. Four different matchers failing on one recording means the fault is in what the model hears, not in the matching. We do not yet know why it hears some recordings and not others. It is not the number of voices: a two-voice Ṛgveda recording decodes cleanly and a two-voice Rudram does not.

What we bring to a collaboration
  • 1,504 lines of Śrī Rudram ghana, each aligned to the second, from two chanters, plus a group recording of the whole Rudram
  • 61.5 hours of Yajurveda and 42 hours of Ṛgveda, aligned word by word to the text
  • The fixed test clips, the scripts for all five approaches, and their results

The goal: a 15-second clip of a chanter who is in none of our recordings is named correctly, on a laptop CPU or a phone, and the system says "I don't know" rather than guess.

2

Hearing a student's svara mistakes audio

A pāṭhaśālā student chants a line, and the tool says which syllables went high or low against the ācārya. Pitch tools are built for singing a melody. A Vedic svara is an accent on one syllable, relative to its neighbours, and the ācārya's reference is often two chanters in unison, which a standard pitch tracker cannot follow.

Our first prototype, on the ācārya's own recording6 / 26syllables where its reading agreed with the text
Current method, mistakes planted in a recorded line100% · 75%raised and lowered svaras caught; median timing error 0.04 s
Lines longer than eight syllablesnot measuredthe median Chamaka line is 18 syllables, the longest 42

The planted-error test is a good start, but it was measured on one student recording with short lines and artificial mistakes. Real students make real mistakes on long lines, and nobody has yet shown a method that catches them.

What we bring to a collaboration
  • Recordings of Śrī Rudram and Chamakam, with 1,504 Rudram lines and 685 Chamakam lines aligned to the second
  • A tool that turns any recorded take into test material, with known mistakes planted in it
  • The methods tried so far and their scores

The goal: on a phone, in real students' recordings, flag the syllables an ācārya would correct, and leave alone the ones an ācārya would not.

3

Hearing every verse in a mixed-language discourse audio

In an upanyāsa the speaker moves from Tamil to chanted Sanskrit and back, sometimes in mid-sentence. Speech models transcribe each language well on its own, but where the speaker switches they can silently drop whole stretches, and nothing in their output says so.

Virāṭa Parva290 versesnever written down by the transcription pass
One Kārtika discourse4 min 27 sof alternating Sanskrit and Tamil with no transcript at all, including the hymn a listener asked for
Across 454 recordings3,557 versesexpected and missing, in 707 stretches totalling 21.7 hours
Placing the known text straight into those stretches1,076 / 5,525half-verses that pass both of our checks

When we know which text is being chanted, we can place it directly into the audio. When we do not, or when the speaker paraphrases, we cannot. Language identification does not find the gaps either: in two of three stretches a listener confirmed as chant, it labelled the audio "mixed" rather than Sanskrit.

What we bring to a collaboration
  • 190 discourses in Tamil, Sanskrit and English, with their transcripts timed to the second
  • An independent language-identification pass over all of them, and the list of missing stretches with their times
  • Placements a listener has confirmed by ear, to test against

The goal: a transcript of a mixed Tamil–Sanskrit discourse that misses no chanted verse, checked against the audio rather than against the model's own output.

4

Find the moment someone remembers search

People remember a discourse by its story, not by its words: "the verse the devas chanted after Jalandhara was killed." They ask in Tamil, English or Sanskrit, and they are usually a little wrong. In that case the hymn is to the Ādiśaktis, not to Śiva. The answer should be the recording, playing at the right second.

The material is ready. 190 recorded discourses have 32,200 transcribed sentences, and every one is timed to the second by forced alignment. 19,545 of them are the speaker's own Tamil explanation. We tested four ways of searching it on 13 questions with known answers, plus 3 questions whose answer is not in the recordings:

Finding the right chapter10–11 / 13mostly solved, by a language model reading the chapter headings or the text
Playing the right moment first8 / 13best result, from searching by meaning across languages
Saying "not here" when it is not0 / 3that same search always answers. No score threshold separates right answers from wrong ones.

The two kinds of search fail on different questions. Meaning-based search crosses languages but misses names written in another script. Sound-based matching catches names like Jalandhara, जलन्धर and ஜலந்தரன் as one word, but cannot connect "seeds" to बीजानि. And some answers were never transcribed at all, which is problem 3.

What we bring to a collaboration
  • The timed transcripts of 190 discourses: Tamil, Sanskrit and English, with the source text each one expounds
  • A test set of 36 remembered-context questions across six series, with answers written as times, plus the scoring harness
  • The four searches and their scores, so a new idea can be compared on the same footing

The goal: a question asked in any of the three languages starts the right recording within 30 seconds of the answer, and returns nothing for a question the recordings do not answer.

Already solved, and in use

  • Citations found by their words, not their numbers. 611 of Kane's footnotes are located by matching the Sanskrit he quotes against 493,992 verses. A shuffled control finds none.
  • The text follows the chant. Our own forced alignment gives word timing on 1,023 Ṛgveda sūktas, and it found recordings cut in the wrong place on both Veda sites.
  • No model is asked what time it is. In the Purāṇa Atlas the model supplies only the words. Every timestamp comes from the audio itself or from a person who listened.
  • OCR that keeps svara marks and conjuncts, used for the Yajurveda and for seven volumes of mixed Sanskrit and Tamil in the Smṛtimuktāphalam.

If any of these problems interests you or your group, write to secretary@sanatanasampatti.in and we can talk about how to work on it together.

Section VII · What you would be funding

The thinking behind the sites

The sites are the visible part. These four documents say what problem they solve, what is proposed next, and how the machinery is built. Start with the first: it is the clearest statement of why this work matters.

Problem statement

How the Index Gets Made

A story can be retold accurately for a century and still lose the one thing that makes it checkable: its address in the text. This note sets out what is missing between the wikis, which give stories without addresses, and machine aggregation, which gives addresses without judgement.

  1. Retelling keeps the story and loses the address.
  2. Machines supply addresses at scale, but cannot tell one sense of a name from another.
  3. The missing step is trained reading, recorded as signed decisions.
  4. That reading can be coursework: studying a verse closely is the same act as ruling on it.
  5. Where a recording exists, the finished citation plays.
Read the problem statement
The example it opens with

A well-known encyclopaedia tells how Satyabhāmā was reborn as Kṛṣṇa's queen, "through her purity of life, austerities and devotion to Viṣṇu". It cites no Purāṇa, no chapter and no verse.

The passage stands at Kārtika Māsa Māhātmya 13. Its Sanskrit heading gives the cause as कार्तिकव्रतपुण्येन, by the merit of the Kārtika observance. In a text written to glorify that one observance, the retelling has dissolved it into general piety.

Without the verse address, nobody could see that this had happened. On the Purāṇa Atlas the verse has an address and a recording that plays it.

Section X · Not yet covered

What we have not done

Naming the gaps makes this a scoped purchase rather than a sales pitch.

  • The core smṛtis. Manu, Yājñavalkya, Āpastamba and Gautama are not in our index, so Kane's most-cited works cannot yet be located. This is the first result on the funding page.
  • Sāmaveda. The Jaiminīya śākhā is in partner collaboration. Other śākhās and seven-svara analysis remain open.
  • Atharvaveda audio. Recordings barely exist outside specialist circles.
  • Āgamas. 277 Śaiva, 77 Śākta and 108 Vaiṣṇava, and none are systematically digitised.
  • Darśanas beyond Advaita. Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya, Yoga and Mīmāṃsā are all under-served.

Nine sites open today. One shared index. The work continues regardless; with you, it becomes an institution.
